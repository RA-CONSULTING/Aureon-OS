Gary Leckey
RA-Consulting
Independent Research
Belfast, United Kingdom
gaxlec@gmail.com
Abstract—This paper presents a theoretical framework and critical feasibility analysis for Project Alfie, a novel ground-based launch system concept. The system is designed to propel a 3,000 kg payload to low Earth orbit using a hybrid propulsion method that synergistically combines staged electromagnetic acceleration with acoustic levitation and stabilisation. The proposed architecture employs a ten-stage linear electromagnetic accelerator housed within a 50-metre vertical shaft, augmented by acoustic transducer arrays for contactless payload centring and vibration damping. A complete first-principles analysis derives the required system parameters, including a magnetic pressure-derived field strength of 24.2 T per stage, acoustic radiation pressure of 12,000 Pa, a net acceleration of 608,400 m/s squared, and gross power demands of 14.2 TW at 50 percent system efficiency. Each parameter is benchmarked against current state-of-the-art capabilities to produce a quantified technology gap assessment. The required field strength of 24.2 T is already below the demonstrated continuous-field record of 45 T, though the 1.0 m bore diameter requirement represents a 31-fold scaling challenge beyond current pulsed magnet apertures. The analysis identifies critical feasibility barriers and proposes a phased development roadmap, contextualised within the broader Electro-Plasma Acoustic Shield (EPAS) research programme where preliminary experimental observations using Bluetooth Low Energy carrier signals at harmonically-derived frequencies have demonstrated measurable electromagnetic field interaction effects. This dual-application analysis demonstrates that while the direct-to-orbit launch configuration remains beyond present engineering capabilities, the underlying physics principles are sound, the technology gaps are quantifiable rather than fundamental, and a staged development pathway exists through progressively demanding applications.
Keywords—electromagnetic launch systems, acoustic levitation, hybrid propulsion, space access technology
I. Introduction
Access to low Earth orbit (LEO) remains one of the most resource-intensive activities in engineering. Contemporary chemical rocket propulsion, while operationally proven, suffers from fundamental thermodynamic limitations imposed by the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, requiring that the majority of launch mass be dedicated to propellant rather than payload [1]. This constraint drives launch costs that, despite recent advances in reusability, remain in the range of 1,500 to 5,000 USD per kilogram to LEO [2].
Electromagnetic launch (EML) concepts offer a fundamentally different approach by accelerating payloads using ground-based infrastructure rather than on-board propellant. Early proposals by O’Neill [3] for lunar mass drivers and subsequent development of electromagnetic railgun technology by the United States Navy [4] have demonstrated that electromagnetic acceleration of macroscopic objects to velocities exceeding 2,000 m/s is achievable. However, no existing system has demonstrated the combination of velocity, payload mass, and trajectory control required for orbital insertion.
This paper presents Project Alfie, a theoretical framework for a staged, hybrid electromagnetic-acoustic direct-to-orbit launch system developed within the broader Electro-Plasma Acoustic Shield (EPAS) research programme [5]. The EPAS framework integrates three complementary physical mechanisms — electromagnetic field interaction, plasma-mediated energy transfer, and acoustic radiation pressure — into a unified architecture governed by a coupled oscillator model termed the Harmonic Nexus Core (HNC).
