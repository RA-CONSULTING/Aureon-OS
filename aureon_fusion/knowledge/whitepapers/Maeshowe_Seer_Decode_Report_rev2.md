## 1. The Dual-Voice Hypothesis

The runic inscriptions at Maeshowe, Orkney, are conventionally interpreted as Viking-age graffiti deposited by Norse warriors during the 12th century CE. This document presents an alternative reading: the inscriptions constitute a deliberate archive from a failing oral knowledge tradition, structured as a dual-voice transmission protocol that requires simultaneous multi-inscription cross-reference to decode.
The hypothesis rests on three independently verifiable claims derived from the primary archaeological sources:
Claim 1 (Epigraphic): The twig-rune cipher system used at Maeshowe (Barnes Nr.9, Nr.20) is inherently a two-channel encoding. Left twigs encode the aett (rune family, 1-3). Right twigs encode position within the aett (1-8). Neither channel alone produces a rune. The decoded rune emerges from the interaction of both readings (Barnes 1994; Nordby 2014; Wikipedia: Cipher runes).
Claim 2 (Spatial): The inscription positions are non-randomly distributed relative to the winter solstice light axis. The highest-authority content (master carver certification Nr.20, dragon visual key Nr.XXXII, unread cipher Nr.15, crusader provenance Nr.11) clusters on the NE shadow wall at the solstice light terminus. The complete alphabet seed-bank sits at the SW passage entrance where light originates. This spatial structure has not been analysed in any published study.
Claim 3 (Acoustic): The 4.57m square chamber produces standing waves. Two men who entered in 1153 were recorded in the Orkneyinga Saga as having gone insane. The Old Norse term used, óðusk, derives from óðr, cognate with Óðinn, indicating ecstatic or divinely-seized states rather than simple fear.
